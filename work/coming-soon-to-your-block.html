Coming Soon to Your Block

What two rows of city data told me about the street outside a bedroom window. Built on NYC Film Permits: a short film, a redesigned No Parking sign, and an app and a text alert so a block knows before the trucks arrive.

What the data showed

NYC Film Permits is published by the Mayor's Office of Media and Entertainment on NYC Open Data and updated daily. It holds streets, dates and hours for every shoot in the city. Not one production name.

580+times one block was held. West 48th Street between 6th and 7th Avenues appears in more than 580 permits since 2023.

Who is affected

  • Students and night-shift workers. Need the block quiet at a specific hour. Find out from a paper sign, if they walk past it.
  • Parents and people working from home. Lights in the windows, generators past midnight, a curb they can't park on.
  • Shop staff and delivery riders. Depend on the block but don't live on it, so no notice ever reaches them.
  • The productions. Fewer surprised neighbors means fewer complaints and fewer calls to the city.

What they do today: notice the sign, ask on Nextdoor, call 311 during the shoot, move the car at the last minute.

Monitor Street, Greenpoint

One block of brick walk-ups between Greenpoint Avenue and Norman Avenue. A deli, a lamppost, bedroom windows facing the street. Held for filming on Thursday until 11:30 PM and again on Friday until 1:00 AM.

The neighborhood is one productions return to. So is this block.

Someone already knew

Permit lead times, from the Film Office rules: applications two business days ahead, signs and notices posted at least 48 hours before. The city knew on Tuesday. The pole knew two days before. The person behind the window found out at 10:52 PM.

Quiet on Set

The night from the inside: a student, an exam at 9 AM, a film shoot outside until 1 AM. Based on the two real permits for Monitor Street, June 25 and 26, 2026.

The problem was never the noise. A film shoot has a permit to make noise. The problem is that the only person on the block who didn't know it was coming was the one living behind the window.

Know before the trucks arrive

Coming Soon to Your Block is built on NYC Film Permits. The sign uses the Film Office template productions must already post. The app and the text read the same rows the city publishes.

One night, five screens

26 screens in the Figma prototype, including workers' access by presence, a shop owner mode, and a neighbors-only screen for visitors. These five follow the night.

Who gets in, and how

The address is converted to a block ID on the phone and not stored. Visitors see only that a permit is active, which is already public record.

For the block, the shops, and no app at all

Block history and feedback are the counts a community board can take to the Film Office. Text alerts use the number printed on the sign.

How it was made

Claude generated the first-pass screens. I refined and rebuilt them in Figma. I shot and edited the film in Premiere Pro.

What the data can tell us, and what it can't

It can tell us

  • Where the curb is held, to the block face
  • When, to the minute, including shoots past midnight
  • How often a block gets used, and which blocks repeat
  • What type of permit it is

It can't tell us

  • What was being filmed
  • Whether the paper notices went up
  • How it felt from inside the apartment
  • Whether anyone on the block wanted a heads-up

Next: interviews with residents, shop owners and night-shift workers on Monitor Street, and a test of the night-before alert with five neighbors. Neither has happened yet.

Somewhere, Monitor Street is on a screen, playing a place it isn't. Nobody who lives there knows which one.
